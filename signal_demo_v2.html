Q1 · Problem Solving + First Principle Thinking
Scenario: Payment success rate dropped from 94% to 87% over 2 weeks. No code changes. Support volume up 23%. A competitor launched 10 days ago. What do you do?
"First I'd separate signal from noise. The fact that there are no code deployments rules out our systems as the root cause — that's actually reassuring. The timing overlap with the competitor launch is suspicious but not causal by itself. I'd start by looking at where the failure is happening — is it a specific payment method, a specific bank, a geography? If it's distributed across all payment rails, the competitor hypothesis becomes more relevant — maybe users are abandoning mid-flow to try the competitor. If it's concentrated in one rail, it's likely a bank-side or PSP issue. I'd pull the failed transaction logs within the hour, segment by failure code, and talk to our PSP contacts in parallel. The 23% support spike suggests users are confused, not that they've left — which tells me something is failing visibly, not silently. I'd get a fix-or-explain answer within 24 hours, not a week."
💡Demonstrated structured decomposition — separated symptoms from causes before proposing action. Strong first-principles approach.
Q2 · Decision Making Under Ambiguity
Scenario: Your team has 2 weeks left in the quarter. You have three features 70% complete. Leadership wants one shipped. Engineering says they're all equal effort. You can't extend the timeline. What do you ship?
"I'd decide in under an hour — not over multiple meetings. The question isn't which feature is technically complete, it's which one has the highest cost of delay. I'd go back to why each feature was prioritised in the first place. Which one was tied to a business metric with a deadline? If feature A was supposed to support a sales motion for Q1, and Q1 starts in 3 weeks, that's the answer. If there's genuinely no external forcing function, I'd ship the one with the narrowest blast radius if something goes wrong — the safest 70% in production is better than the most impressive 70% that takes the site down. I'd communicate the decision clearly and quickly, not hedge. The team can't execute on ambiguity."
💡Arrived at a decision framework rather than hedging. Comfort with incomplete information is evident.
Q3 · Curiosity
Open-ended: Given the payment failure scenario in Q1 — what are the five things you'd want to know more about that weren't in the brief?
"1. What does our churn curve look like for users who hit a failed payment — do they retry or leave? 2. Is our retry logic optimised, or are we just presenting the failure to the user and expecting them to act? 3. What's the failure message users are actually seeing — is it our copy or the bank's error code? 3. Has our PSP changed any of their routing rules in the last 30 days? 4. Do we have real-time visibility into competitor transaction success rates, and if not, why not? 5. Are we measuring payment success rate by transaction or by session — because a user who fails twice and succeeds on the third attempt inflates our failure rate but the outcome was fine."
💡Asked operational, analytical, and UX questions — showed range beyond the obvious. The PSP routing and measurement methodology questions were unusually specific.
